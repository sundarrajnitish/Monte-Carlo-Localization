Where am I? Ask a thousand guesses.

A robot drives down a 28 m hallway. It has a map, wheel odometry that drifts, and a laser scanner that sees walls. It has no idea where it started. Below, a particle filter starts with a few thousand random guesses spread over the whole building and, scan by scan, lets the wrong ones die. This page explains every step of that process with simulations you can drive yourself, and reports what we measured.

01 · THE PROBLEM

A map, a log, and no GPS

Everything on this page runs on one real dataset. The map is an occupancy grid of a hallway: 700 × 85 cells of 4 cm, so 28 m by 3.4 m, with doors and alcoves on both sides. The robot log is 45 seconds of driving: 522 odometry readings and 240 laser scans. Each scan has 180 beams, one per degree from the robot's right to its left, measured in centimetres. The laser sits 25 cm in front of the robot's centre, and beams that see nothing come back at the saturation value 8183 cm.

The catch: odometry is reported in the robot's own odometry frame, which starts wherever the wheels were first switched on. Its origin, and even its heading, have nothing to do with the map. Only differences between two odometry readings mean anything. Localization is the job of finding the transform between the two frames, and keeping it right as the wheels slip.

Before any algorithm, try it by hand. Drag the grey robot until the laser end-points land on the walls. The score is the log-likelihood the filter itself would compute.

02 · BELIEF AS A CLOUD OF PARTICLES

The Bayes filter, one dimension first

The robot never knows its pose xt; it keeps a belief, a probability distribution over all poses. Every step of the Bayes filter does two things:

predict: bel̄(xt) = ∫ p(xt | ut, xt−1) bel(xt−1) dxt−1
correct: bel(xt) = η · p(zt | xt, m) · bel̄(xt)

Motion spreads the belief out (we are less sure after moving); a measurement sharpens it. A particle filter represents the belief with M samples. Predicting means moving every sample through a noisy motion model. Correcting means giving each sample a weight w = p(z | x). Then resampling draws a fresh set, with replacement, in proportion to the weights. Heavy particles are copied; light ones disappear.

Try it on the classic warm-up: a 15 m corridor with doors at 3, 5 and 10 m. The robot only senses "door" or "no door". Start with particles spread evenly, then alternate Move and Sense. Watch the cloud split into several hypotheses and then collapse onto one.

MCL(Xt−1, ut, zt, m):
for m = 1 … M:
x[m] ~ p(xt | ut, x[m]t−1) // motion model
w[m] = p(zt | x[m], m) // sensor model
if Neff = 1 / Σ w² < M/2:
Xt ← low-variance resample(X, w)
return Xt

1-D corridor with three doors

Two lessons carry straight over to the 2-D robot. First, a single measurement can be consistent with several places; the particle cloud holds all of them at once, something a single Gaussian (a Kalman filter) cannot do. Second, only motion breaks the tie: after moving 2 m, "door again" is only plausible for the hypothesis that started at the 3 m door.

03 · PREDICTION

The odometry motion model

Between two odometry readings the robot has done three things: turned by δrot1 to face its direction of travel, driven δtrans, and turned again by δrot2. These three numbers do not depend on the odometry frame, so they can be applied to every particle in the map frame. Each is corrupted with zero-mean noise whose variance grows with the motion:

σ²rot1 = α₁δ²rot1 + α₂δ²trans  ·  σ²trans = α₃δ²trans + α₄(δ²rot1 + δ²rot2)  ·  σ²rot2 = α₁δ²rot2 + α₂δ²trans

Drag the target pose and play with the α's. Rotational noise bends the cloud into the famous banana: a small heading error, carried over a long distance, becomes a large sideways error.

Two corner cases the textbook formula misses. A pure rotation has no direction of travel, so atan2(0, 0) is meaningless; we set δrot1 = 0 and put the whole turn in δrot2. Driving backwards would otherwise be modelled as "spin 180°, drive, spin 180°", which injects enormous rotational noise; we model it as a negative translation instead. Both happen in the real log.
04 · CORRECTION

Two ways to score a laser scan

The sensor model p(z | x, m) answers: if the robot were at pose x in map m, how likely is this scan? Beams are treated as independent, so the scan likelihood is a product over beams. Two classic models:

Beam model: ray-cast, then compare

For every beam, cast a ray through the map to get the range z* the sensor should see, then score the measured z with a mixture of four effects: a Gaussian hit around z*, an exponential short reading (people, chair legs, anything not in the map), a spike at max range (no echo), and a small uniform random floor that keeps a single bad beam from zeroing a particle.

Likelihood field: skip the ray casting

The beam model needs a ray cast per beam per particle, which is expensive, and its score jumps when a ray grazes a corner. The likelihood-field model (end-point model) instead projects each beam's end-point into the map and asks only: how far is it from the nearest obstacle? A distance transform of the map, computed once, answers that with a table lookup:

p(zk | x, m) = zhit · N(dist(end-pointk); 0, σ) + zrand / zmax

Work in log space. 36 beams, each with a density around 10−2, multiply to about 10−72. Do that for 180 beams and double precision underflows to exactly zero for every particle. The filter sums log-likelihoods, subtracts the maximum, and only then exponentiates (log-sum-exp).
Beams are not independent. Neighbouring beams hit the same wall and share the same map errors, so treating 180 of them as independent makes the filter wildly over-confident. Two standard fixes are used here: use every 5th beam (36 per scan), and optionally divide the log-likelihood by a temperature T > 1. The findings show T matters a lot for global localization.
05 · SURVIVAL OF THE LIKELIEST

Resampling without losing diversity

After weighting, most particles carry almost no weight. Resampling draws M new particles, each picked with probability equal to its weight. Lay the normalised weights end to end on [0, 1) and throw pointers at them:

  • Multinomial: M independent random pointers. Simple, but a particle can be lost by bad luck even with a large weight.
  • Low-variance (systematic): one random offset r ∈ [0, 1/M), then a comb of pointers r + m/M. O(M), and a particle with weight w is copied either ⌊Mw⌋ or ⌈Mw⌉ times, never anything wilder.

Resampling is also not free: every resample throws away information and shrinks diversity. So we only do it when the effective sample size Neff = 1/Σw² drops below M/2.

08 · RUN IT YOURSELF

Three implementations, one algorithm

Python (python/mcl) is the reference: vectorised NumPy, unit-tested, and used for every finding on this page. MATLAB (matlab/) is toolbox-free and also runs in GNU Octave; on the real log it agrees with Python to about 1 cm on average. The JavaScript port (docs/js/core.js) powers this page.

All three share the same coordinate frame, parameters and design decisions: log-space weights, adaptive resampling, scan gating (a new scan is only used after 5 cm or 5° of motion, so a parked robot does not become over-confident by re-reading the same scan), and a robust pose estimate that averages only the dominant mode, because the plain weighted mean of a two-mode belief sits between the modes, usually inside a wall.

# Python 3.10+
git clone https://github.com/sundarrajnitish/Monte-Carlo-Localization
cd Monte-Carlo-Localization/python
pip install -r requirements.txt
python -m pytest -q                    # unit tests
python scripts/run_localization.py     # animate the real log
python scripts/run_experiments.py      # every number on this page

% MATLAB (no toolboxes) or GNU Octave
cd matlab
run_mcl        % filter the real log, animated
test_mcl       % self-checks
